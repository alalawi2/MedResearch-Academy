Handover language · A fictional exercise

Say what is known.
Leave the gap visible.

When a colleague asks for a quick update, it can feel easier to offer an answer than to explain that a detail is still unconfirmed. Clear wording can make that uncertainty explicit.

Written by the Wardwise project team · 27 October 2026. This is an original language exercise awaiting independent clinical and educator review.

The case facts

Taylor is a fictional patient in room 4.

Taylor has asked when the team will visit.

No visit time has been confirmed. No clinical findings are supplied.

Your colleague asks: “Can you give me a quick update?” In this exercise, your task is to pass on Taylor’s question and distinguish the known information from the missing detail.

Compare two responses

Adds an unsupported promise

“Taylor is waiting. The team will come soon.”

The word “soon” introduces an expectation that is not in the case facts. It also leaves out the question Taylor asked.

Keeps the uncertainty explicit

“Taylor in room 4 has asked when the team will visit. A time hasn’t been confirmed, so I haven’t given Taylor a time.”

This version names the question and explains which detail is missing. It does not suggest that you know when the team will arrive.

A few phrases to rehearse

  • “The time hasn’t been confirmed.”
  • “I don’t have that information yet.”
  • “Taylor has asked whether there is an update.”

These phrases do different jobs. The first describes an unconfirmed arrangement. The second describes what you know. The third passes on a question. Choose wording that matches the facts you actually have rather than memorising a sentence for every situation.

Your turn

  1. Cover the sample response and read only the three case facts.
  2. Say or write a brief update to your colleague.
  3. Read it back. Did you include Taylor’s question? Did you add a time, a promise or a clinical fact that was not supplied?
  4. Try the sentence again, keeping the uncertainty explicit.

There is no single required wording here. The point of the exercise is to notice what your sentence communicates, then make a deliberate revision. If you record yourself, use only these fictional details.

This page is not a complete clinical handover template, SBAR protocol or instruction about patient care. In real work, follow your organisation’s handover and escalation requirements. Do not use this exercise to decide what clinical information to include.
